 
 
 Quantitative Analysis of Urban Location Characteristics, Environmental Im-
pacts, and Policy Strategy Effects on Mega Sporting Events 
Sport mega-events create substantial social and economic value but also impose significant envi-
ronmental costs. Therefore, it is of great importance to understand the environmental impacts of these 
events and to explore how changes in their planning may alter these impacts. 
Firstly, taking Super Bowl LIX in New Orleans as the baseline, 17 quantitative indicators covering 
both environmental impacts and city location characteristics are established. Reasonable value ranges 
and physical interpretations specified based on published literature and statistical data. In addition, 8 
sub-criteria are introduced to capture sustainability factors relevant to multi-sport competition.  
Secondly, the relationship between city location characteristics and environmental impacts is 
quantitatively characterized. City features are first reduced by principal component analysis (PCA) 
based on Pearson correlations, and a multiple linear regression model is then constructed with the 
principal component scores as explanatory variables, yielding a PCA-based urban environmental 
impact model. Three latent dimensions—infrastructure level (PC1), climate–geographical conditions 
(PC2), and population density (PC3)—jointly explain about 85% of the total variance. Grid carbon 
intensity, renewable energy share, public transit coverage, and the share of green or low-carbon travel 
are identified as the main structural drivers of environmental performance. 
Thirdly, an environmental impact evaluation model is developed within this multi-indicator 
framework. ARIMA models are applied to forecast future city characteristics for U.S. cities that have 
already hosted the Super Bowl and to project 2029 features for three NFL cities that have not yet 
hosted—Charlotte, Buffalo, and Kansas City. The forecasted features are then input into the principal 
component regression model to obtain future environmental indicators, which are further aggregated 
into composite scores using the entropy-weight method. The results indicate that Santa Clara ex-
hibits the best environmental performance among historical hosts, while Kansas City attains the high-
est score among the three potential new hosts. 
Fourthly, the model is extended to comprehensive mega-events such as the Summer Olympic 
Games. Building on the single-event evaluation framework, additional indicators reflecting the sus-
tainability of multi-venue, multi-sport events are introduced, and a composite venue evaluation 
model based on principal component regression is developed . The results show that for single 
events, environmental pressure is concentrated mainly in short-term peaks in water use and spectator 
transport emissions, whereas for multi-venue, multi-sport events,  long-term effects  from Olympic 
Village energy consumption, venue network layout, and post-event venue reuse play a more dominant 
role. After applying the entropy-weight method to derive composite environmental scores, Los Ange-
les emerges as the best-performing host under the comprehensive event scenario.  
Finally, a sensitivity analysis is conducted to quantify the influence of key policy variables on 
environmental impacts. The infrastructure investment, energy procurement, and transport-mode shifts 
are abstracted as three policy control variables and mapped, via interpretable transfer functions, 
to changes in city characteristics and environmental indicators. The resulting effects are then evaluated 
using an environmental composite score within the broader assessment model, confirming the robust-
ness of the approach.

1 Introduction 
1.1 Problem Background 
As sport mega-events such as the Super Bowl and the Olympic Games generate substantial social 
and economic benefits, they also produce significant environmental impacts, especially in terms of 
energy use and carbon emissions. With the global push toward carbon neutrality and sustainable de-
velopment, the tension between sport mega-events and environmental protection has become increas-
ingly pronounced. 
In recent years, organizing committees and third-party institutions have attempted to quantify the 
environmental impacts of these events through carbon-footprint accounting and environmental rating 
systems. However, the resulting scores are still used mainly for post-event summaries and image pro-
motion. They are rarely incorporated into key decision stages such as host-city selection and infra-
structure planning, so environmental impacts continue to receive insufficient weight in practice. 
Against this background, it is necessary to construct a mathematical model that explicitly aims to 
minimize environmental impacts and enables meaningful comparison across different potential host 
cities. Taking the Super Bowl as a case study, this paper analyzes the structure of its environmental 
footprint and develops an evaluation model for environmentally sustainable venue selection, providing 
quantitative and operational decision support for organizing committees. 
1.2 Restatement of the Problem 
(1) Analyze the environmental impacts of the Super Bowl in New Orleans, focusing on energy, 
emissions, water use, and waste, and show how changing the host city would affect these factors.  
(2) Develop a model that evaluates Super Bowl host cities based on city characteristics (electricity 
mix, climate, transport) and event parameters (attendance, energy demand, travel). 
(3) Apply the model to historical Super Bowl hosts and select the most sustainable city for 2029, 
while evaluating three new NFL cities. 
(4) Extend the model to other mega-events like the Olympics, considering factors like event du-
ration, venue networks, and international travel. 
(5) Summarize findings in a 1–2-page letter to the NFL, recommending the most sustainable 2029 
host city and explaining how this model aligns with their sustainability goals. 
1.3 Our work 
In this paper, we develop a data-driven framework for evaluating the environmental performance 
of Super Bowl host cities. We construct a two-layer indicator system that separates event-level out-
comes (energy use, emissions, water, and waste) from city-level drivers (climate, transport, and energy 
mix), using New Orleans as a reference case. We apply principal component analysis (PCA) and prin-
cipal component regression (PCR) to link city characteristics to environmental indicators. We forecast 
city data to 2029 using ARIMA, aggregate the indicators into a sustainability score via entropy 
weighting, and apply the model to historical Super Bowl cities and new NFL cities, ranking them on 
environmental performance. Finally, we extend the framework to the Summer Olympics and conduct 
sensitivity analysis on infrastructure, energy, and transport before presenting a recommendation to the 
NFL.

2 Assumptions and Justifications 
To simplify the problem, we make the following basic assumptions, each of which is properly 
justified. 
 Assumption 1: The historical climatic conditions of each candidate city during the Super 
Bowl period are relatively stable. 
Justification: This assumption presupposes that while there are regional differences in tempera-
ture and precipitation levels among candidate cities during the event period (typically February), the 
fluctuations in their historical data fall within a reasonable range and can serve as stability parameters 
in the model. 
 Assumption 2: The urban population flow patterns remain stable. 
Justification: Based on the regularities of historical data on urban population migration, this as-
sumption holds that each candidate city maintains relatively stable population mobility characteristics 
on an annual time scale. 
 Assumption 3: Reasonable extrapolation can be conducted based on historical data trends. 
Justification: Due to practical constraints on data availability, the quantitative analysis in this 
paper is mainly constructed based on historical observation data up to 2024. We assume that the basic 
correlation patterns between urban characteristics and environmental impacts revealed by current data, 
the trajectory characteristics of infrastructure development, and the evolution trends of energy structure 
will remain consistent in the short term. 
 Assumption 4: Candidate cities can maintain system stability within the prediction period. 
Justification: We assume that within the model’s prediction time horizon (up to 2029), none of 
the candidate cities will experience major sudden events that could significantly alter their infrastruc-
ture characteristics, environmental carrying capacity, or population distribution patterns.

Water use is mainly reflected in venue-related consumption and per-capita usage, with toilet

In terms of solid waste, a regular NFL game produces about 40 tons of waste, whereas the Super 
Bowl generates nearly 60 tons [2]. The recycling/composting rate within this total is a key measure of 
waste management performance: a low rate implies that more recoverable materials are landfilled or 
incinerated, worsening resource loss and environmental pollution. 
Greenhouse-gas emissions from the Super Bowl mainly come from transportation, spectator ac-
tivities, and material promotion. Cross-regional travel by tens of thousands of spectators generates 
roughly 35,000 tons of CO₂e, while the advertising segment alone is estimated to emit about 2 million 
tons of CO₂—comparable to the annual emissions of around 1 million Americans. These figures show 
that greenhouse-gas emissions are a central component of the event’s overall environmental footprint 
and have a significant cumulative impact on climate.

4 PCA-Based Multiple Linear Regression Model of Environmental Impacts 
Firstly, we use Pearson correlations to build a matrix including city features and environmental 
impact indicators to identify the linear relationships and potential multicollinearity. Secondly, we apply 
principal component analysis (PCA) on the standardized city features matrix, replacing highly cor-
related variables with principal-component scores. Lastly, we regress each environmental indicator 
on the selected component scores via ordinary least squares and evaluate fit using statistics such as 
𝑹𝟐, and RMSE. This approach provides a stable, interpretable mapping from “city features” to “envi-
ronmental impacts” even with limited samples.

“International airport throughput” reflects a city’s node rank and carrying capacity within the 
global air network and thus has structural significance for the environmental impacts of comprehensive 
multi-sport events. Including it as a site characteristic helps capture system-level global accessibility—
an environmental factor rarely considered for single-sport events that are largely fed by domestic flows. 
Compared with single-venue events, a defining feature of comprehensive games is the high fre-

the mean inter-venue travel distance becomes a key structural variable affecting transport energy use

The in-Games venue reuse rate measures, within a single edition, the extent to which the same

spectator routing, and a practical metric for sustainability planning during the Games.

and how long facilities will continue to be used after the Games. A higher reutilization rate implies 
lower hidden costs and a stronger legacy, turning a short-term event into an ongoing environmental 
stewardship responsibility for the host city.

7 Comparative Analysis of Model Results 
By comparing the results from Sections 3–4 with those from Section 5, several structural differ-
ences emerge between single-event mega-events such as the Super Bowl and large-scale, multi-venue 
or multi-sport competitions such as the Summer Olympic Games. The key differentiating mechanisms 
can be summarized as follows: 
(1) Temporal Structure: Short-Term Peaks vs. Long-Horizon Accumulation 
In the Super Bowl model (Sections 3–4), the entropy-weighted results assign the highest weights 
to stadium water use, per-capita water consumption, spectator travel emissions, and transportation-
related emissions, followed by electricity consumption for stadiums and hotels. Construction-phase 
and long-term legacy indicators contribute almost nothing to the weighting structure. This pattern in-
dicates that the environmental pressure of a single-event competition is dominated by short-duration, 
peak-load consumption concentrated around game day. In contrast, in the Olympic model, indicators 
such as Olympic Village electricity intensity and construction/demolition waste obtain significantly 
higher entropy weights. This shift reflects that multi-venue events generate cumulative, time-extended 
sustainability pressure, where long-horizon resource use becomes more influential than short-term op-
erational spikes. 
(2) Spatial Structure: Single-Venue Events vs. Multi-Venue Networks 
In the Super Bowl model, spatially relevant factors are primarily: Average spectator travel dis-
tance, Public transport coverage, Share of green transportation. Regression results show that these 
variables strongly explain spectator and transportation-related carbon emissions. In the Olympic model, 
newly added variables—average inter-venue travel distance, in-Games venue reuse rate, and post-
Games reutilization rate—form a distinct principal component (PC3), interpreted as the “venue spatial 
configuration and legacy utilization” dimension. This component contributes meaningfully to multiple 
environmental indicators. This comparison highlights that for comprehensive multi-sport events, the 
spatial network of venues, together with the multi-tier mobility system linking venues and the Olympic 
Village, becomes a structural driver of environmental outcomes. 
(3) Shared Core Drivers: Electricity Systems and Transport Structure 
Across both models, a consistent result emerges: Electricity carbon intensity, Renewable-en-
ergy share, Public transport coverage, and Green mobility proportion.All exhibit high importance 
in both the principal component regressions and the entropy weighting scheme. These variables func-
tion as common core determinants of environmental performance. However, the mechanisms differ. 
For the Super Bowl, these city attributes primarily amplify or mitigate single-day carbon footprints via 
short-term electricity demand and transportation organization. For the Olympic Games, they determine 
not only operational emissions during the 17-day event window but also the long-term emission base-
line for decades of venue and Olympic Village operation.

9 Strength and Limitations 
The merit of our model lies in its scientifically grounded indicator system and the integration of 
multiple analytical methods. By combining indicators for energy use, water, waste, transport emissions, 
and the urban environment, it offers a multi-dimensional yet intuitive way to evaluate the Super Bowl’s 
environmental impact. The PCA-based regression model mitigates multicollinearity and extracts three 
stable principal components explaining over 85% of total variance, giving both statistical robustness 
and clear interpretation. ARIMA forecasting combined with PCA regression allows forward-looking 
evaluations of candidate host cities without requiring future observations, while the entropy-weight 
method enhances objectivity by assigning greater weight to more discriminative indicators. Scenario-
based sensitivity analysis further shows how policy changes—such as expanded renewable energy or 
shifts in transport structure—alter projected impacts, making the framework useful for long-term sus-
tainability planning. 
However, the framework has limitations. The regression is calibrated on a relatively small set of 
historical host cities, which constrains statistical power and generalizability. Several indicators rely on 
simplified assumptions, such as multi-year climate averages and linear mobility approximations, 
which may bias results for cities with high variability or rapid structural change. ARIMA forecasting 
assumes stable trends and does not capture non-linear shocks from policy shifts, infrastructure up-
grades, economic change, or extreme climate events, potentially distorting projections for 2029. The 
assessment also focuses mainly on operational impacts, only partially accounting for embodied carbon 
in stadium construction and long-term energy use from subsequent events, so the full life-cycle foot-
print is understated. Finally, while entropy weighting is objective, it ignores expert judgment and may 
underemphasize important but low-variance indicators; future work that blends objective and expert-
based weights could improve realism and balance.

On behalf of the HiMCM Super Bowl Sustainability Consultants (SSC), we are honored to present

on environmental impact, our team evaluated how different cities would perform if they hosted the 
game in 2029. 
In our study, we compared candidate cities on key dimensions such as event-related energy use, 
greenhouse-gas emissions, water consumption, and solid-waste generation, together with city factors 
including climate, electricity mix, transportation options, and existing sustainability practices. Using

 Lower-carbon energy and mild climate : A relatively clean regional grid and temperate February

that Santa Clara generates a smaller projected footprint while still meeting the operational needs of a
