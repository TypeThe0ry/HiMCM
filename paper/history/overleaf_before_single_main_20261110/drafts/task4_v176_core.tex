\section{Data collection and comparability}
\subsection{A traceable city--indicator matrix}
We compare $M=16$ cities on $N=10$ indicators covering safety, environment,
household costs, education and everyday resources. The initial matrix is the
team-supplied city table \cite{teamtable}. We retain its existing values, populations, reference
years and source flags as the agreed input, then supplement only empty cells.
Some supplied values lack original source URLs, so their provenance remains
the team table; official supplements do not retrospectively validate every
supplied measurement. Table~\ref{tab:indicators} states what each variable measures.

\input{task4_v154_assets/indicators.tex}

The nominal reference year is 2025, but the evidence is mixed-vintage. Every
supplement retains its actual year rather than being relabelled as 2025.
Population denominators remain those of the supplied table. Thus a 2026 facility
snapshot divided by a supplied 2025 population is a conditional density, not an
observed same-year census. We do not infer that facilities are unchanged merely
because they may change slowly. Historical inventory assumptions are evaluated
through withdrawal scenarios.

We use official statistical tables, operator inventories and municipal geodata
to fill missing fields. For each admitted supplement, the archive records the
provider, URL, year, geographic and population scope, units, extraction rule and
file hash. Original PDFs and API responses are retained separately from derived
tables. Search summaries locate evidence; numerical admission requires the
underlying record or an explicitly identified provider capture. Values are
converted to compatible units before utility calculation: for example, the
supplied higher-education density per million is divided by ten to obtain the
per-100,000 scale. Mode share is not converted into physical stop density.

\subsection{Collection problems and their resolution}
\textbf{Count city facilities, not platforms or entire networks.}
Selecting Oslo's municipal GIS records within the city boundary yields 89
metro stations after consolidating 186
platforms; Zurich's 2025 VBZ report distinguishes 446 city stops from 235
outside the city \cite{oslo,vbz}. Helsinki's November 2026 operator snapshot
similarly separates 19 Helsinki stations from 11 in Espoo \cite{helsinki}.
For a verified subset count $c$, fixed population $P$ and anchor $a$, we use
$d=10^5c/P$ and utility $[d/(a+d),1]$, leaving uncounted providers unknown.
Madrid's May 2026 register has 293 records, of which 242 carry city code
28079. Omitting two unnamed records without lines and consolidating native
station identifiers gives 191 complexes \cite{madridmetro,inecodes}. Vienna illustrates why record totals
alone are insufficient: 1,695 named areas include unverified depot objects,
so only 99 metro areas individually matched to existing-station geodata and
the city polygon are retained \cite{viennaogd,viennagis}.

Native identifiers, verified aliases and operator interchange maps prevent
transfer stations from being counted once per line. Prague retains 57 current
metro complexes; Flora's closure explains its omission from the 58 distinct
complexes in the 2025 report, whereas Florenc's partial accessibility restriction
does not close the whole complex \cite{pidmetro,dpp2025,floraclose}.
Berlin's nine BVG U-Bahn profiles consolidate 131 zone-A appearances into 100
complexes. Zone A extends through the S-Bahn ring; the retained U-Bahn subset
leaves outer stations and other modes uncounted
\cite{bvg}. Milan's municipal layer, distinguished from its entrance layer,
selects records with both the Milano name and code 015146, yielding 104
stations/hubs after five map-supported interchange consolidations;
proximity alone does not merge stations \cite{milanmetro}.
London's inventory mixes platforms and entrances with stations. From 272
station-level records, 62 have exact fare-zone value 1; it retains 59
stations/hubs after two conservative omissions and
Paddington hub consolidation; representative coordinates lie within the ONS
December 2025 London-region boundary \cite{tflinventory,onslondon}.
Singapore's LTA GeoJSON contributes 5,205 distinct nonempty bus-stop codes,
without distance clustering \cite{sgbus_lta}; Stockholm's 2025 city--police
report identifies thirteen metro stations in two districts \cite{stockholmmetro2025}.

\textbf{Keep inventory dates, status and venue units explicit.}
Milan's layer is dated 16 October 2024; Paris's 244 intra-muros metro stations
come from a March 2022 atlas using RATP 2021 and Apur 2022 sources
\cite{milanmetro,apur}. These historical lower-bound proxies are not relabelled
as 2025 counts; a separate withdrawal restores the Paris supplement to unknown.
Vienna, Berlin and London use November 2026 inventories. All retain the supplied
population denominators, including Paris's 2023, Dublin's 2022 and
Singapore's 2024 values. Inventory membership
does not establish continuous operation or adolescent access.
Stockholm's November 2026 directory retains thirteen municipal swimming venues after excluding a
renovation closure and counting its replacement once \cite{stockpool}.
Tokyo retains five Nerima indoor venues at distinct addresses: Heiwadai's
current closure excludes it, while Miharadai's announced February--May 2027
closure is not a current closure \cite{nerimapool,nerimaclose}.
Other named pool subsets contain seven Singapore venues from April 2025,
seven Zurich indoor venues accessed in November 2026, and seventeen Milan
indoor venues under the June 2025 tariff \cite{sgpool_2025,zurichpool,milanpool2025}.
They count physical venues, not basins or lanes, and leave other providers unknown.

\textbf{Match school stages and teaching units before forming ratios.}
Prague's 2024/25 totals are 80,073 secondary-school pupils and 6,877.1
teacher full-time equivalents (FTE), including lower-gymnasium years. Subtracting the matching
8,748 pupils and 737.8 FTE gives 71,325 upper-stage pupils and
a rounding envelope of $[6139.2,6139.4]$ teacher FTE, including adult and non-daytime study modes
\cite{prague}. Stockholm's city-school ratio covers upper-secondary years 1--3, all ownership
types and the 15 October reference date. Its API value 16.42091405 is retained
as $[16.35,16.45]$: a declared precision convention, not a measurement-error
confidence interval \cite{kolada}.
Warsaw's 2024/25 yearbook gives 119,740 pupils across six youth school routes
by school location and all ownership types. Unknown youth allocation within
adult-inclusive general-school FTE gives 2,490--8,144 FTE; outward one-FTE
guards on four published cells widen this to 2,487--8,148 \cite{warsawedu2025}.
The guards encode a unit-rounding assumption. These allocation and rounding
bounds describe school resources, not a resident age-14--18 population or a
statistical confidence interval.

Some published denominators fail these checks. Henri-IV in Paris reports
794 lyc\'ee pupils and 153.65 institution FTE for 2025, without upper-stage
allocation or complete city coverage; a 2026 post-baccalaureate directory flag cannot recover
2025 workloads \cite{deppmatch,deppdirectory}. Dublin's school-roll payroll
FTE are unpublished \cite{irelandfte}; its November 2025 City Of Dublin ETB
post-primary vocational subgroup has 411.8 payroll FTE against 376.1 allocated posts, so
allocations are not a payroll ceiling \cite{irelandpayroll}.
Finland's national FTE combines register, salary and private-provider estimation;
without a verified one-sided error envelope it is not a guaranteed Helsinki
teacher upper bound \cite{finlandfte}. All three city PTRs remain unknown.

\textbf{Preserve incompatible rate units and supplied values.}
Amsterdam's CBS sources give ten provisional victims in 2022 and 68 across
2018--2022, neither matching the supplied numerator 340 \cite{cbshomicide2022}.
Zurich's PKS lists one completed offence in 2024 and two in 2025; offences
count criminal acts independently of victims, and the submitted rate 0.14
still lacks a verified denominator \cite{zhpks2025}. Both original entries
remain, with their contribution tested in Section~\ref{sec:sourcepolicy};
we do not manufacture replacement rates from incompatible units.

\textbf{Retain unobserved travellers and failed retrievals as uncertainty.}
Singapore's initial census-PDF endpoint returned 404; the official Parliament
mirror and API supplied the matching report and table \cite{singapore}.
Duration-bin utility uses endpoints, no midpoints or artificial open-tail cap.
Weighted enrolment estimates exceed travel coverage by 1,811 secondary,
989 post-secondary non-tertiary and 1,635 polytechnic students. These are
weighted survey estimates, not complete headcounts. Uncovered mass
retains $[0,1]$ utility, and unknown stage shares use the interval convex hull.
The resulting $[0.21048,0.61884]$ is a 2020 school-stage proxy, not an identified
2025 interval for ages 14--18.
Dublin's stop-download 403 yielded no catalogue-inferred count; a later official
map from the 2023 plan, issued in 2024, supports nineteen existing
city-centre tram stops \cite{dublin}.
Warsaw's recovered 2022 survey reports a rounded 27-minute school trip for a
city subgroup with sample base 22, but covers residents aged 15 and above
without an upper-secondary or matched 14--18 denominator \cite{warsaw2022}.
Its travel utility remains unknown.

\section{Establishing the baseline evaluation model}
\subsection{Determining indicator weights}
We retain eight main indicators at weight 0.1125 each and two auxiliary facility
indicators, transport stops and pools, at 0.05 each, so $\sum_jw_j=1$.
These weights express a transparent modelling choice. They are not estimated
from a student survey. Equal weights provide a separate reference.
TOPSIS is the primary score; VIKOR provides a focused method comparison.
AHP represents explicit preference scenarios \cite{saaty}, and Monte Carlo measures
sensitivity to declared distributions. These extensions retain the same
criteria rather than introduce unrelated ranking models.

For preference multipliers $m_j>0$, we use
\begin{equation}
 w'_j=\frac{m_jw_j}{\sum_kw_km_k},\qquad A_{jk}=\frac{w'_j}{w'_k}.
 \label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
\begin{samepage}
The principal eigenvector of $A$ recovers $w'$. With $\lambda_{\max}$ its
largest eigenvalue, $CI=(\lambda_{\max}-N)/(N-1)$ and $CR=CI/RI_N$.
Because this matrix is built from exact ratios, $\lambda_{\max}=N$ and
$CR=0$ up to numerical precision. This verifies internal consistency;
it does not establish that these are students' actual preferences.
\par\end{samepage}

\subsection{TOPSIS scoring calculation}
We adapt TOPSIS \cite{topsis} using fixed reference anchors to preserve a common scoring scale across evidence,
preference and future scenarios. For benefit and cost measurements respectively,
\begin{equation}
 u^{+}(x)=\frac{x}{a+x},\qquad u^{-}(x)=\frac{a}{a+x}.
\end{equation}
Each anchor in Table~\ref{tab:indicators} is a declared reference scale, not
an estimated optimum. Travel uses $q(t)=e^{-t/30}$ for duration $t$ in minutes.
When only mean duration $\bar t$ is known, Jensen's inequality gives
$\mathbb E[q(t)]\in[e^{-\bar t/30},1]$. Fully missing utilities remain $[0,1]$.

For utility vector $u_i$, weighted distances from fixed worst and best points are
\begin{equation}
 D_i^-=\sqrt{\sum_jw_ju_{ij}^2},\qquad
 D_i^+=\sqrt{\sum_jw_j(1-u_{ij})^2},\qquad
 S_i=100\frac{D_i^-}{D_i^-+D_i^+}.
\end{equation}
Weights enter the squared distances once. Since $S_i$ increases with every
utility, endpoints $\ell_i,h_i$ give $L_i=S(\ell_i)$ and $U_i=S(h_i)$.
Under the retained rectangular uncertainty set, define
\begin{equation}
 R_i=\max\{0,\max_{k\ne i}U_k-L_i\},\qquad
 i^*\in\arg\min_iR_i,
 \label{eq:regret}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal C=\{i:U_i\geq\max_{k\ne i}L_k\}.
\end{equation}
Maximum regret $R_i$ measures how far the selected city could trail another
city. Set $\mathcal C$ retains every city that could still be first.
The maximum excludes the selected city itself. These conditional data bounds
are not sampling confidence intervals, and a conservative recommendation does
not identify a uniquely best city.

Zurich minimizes maximum regret, followed by Singapore and Oslo

The decision order expresses a conservative recommendation under the stated

\section{Model revision and extension}
\subsection{Considering future changes}
We examine three conditional changes while holding anchors and population
denominators fixed. Resource expansion raises higher-education, stop and pool
densities by 10\%. Living pressure raises living costs, housing cost ratios and
travel durations by 10\%. Balanced improvement reduces pupil--teacher ratios,
PM$_{2.5}$ and travel durations by 10\%. A comparable multi-year panel is not
available, so these changes have no estimated annual rate or forecast date.

For raw multiplier $r>0$, the utility transformations are
\begin{equation}
 T_r^{+}(u)=\frac{ru}{1-u+ru},\qquad
 T_r^{-}(u)=\frac{u}{u+r(1-u)}.
\end{equation}
These increasing maps propagate raw-scale changes through benefit and cost
intervals. For known mean travel duration, the new Jensen interval is
$[e^{-r\bar t/30},1]$. Singapore instead has bounds $[L_q,U_q]$ on expected
utility. With $q=e^{-t/30}$ and $t'=rt$, Jensen and $0\leq q\leq1$ give
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb E[q^r]\in
 \begin{cases}
 [L_q,U_q^r],&0<r<1,\\
 [L_q,U_q],&r=1,\\
 [L_q^r,U_q],&r>1.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:moment}
\end{equation}
Raising both endpoints to $r$ would generally misrepresent an expected utility.
Equation~\ref{eq:moment} is an outer moment bound and need not be reversible.
Fully missing travel remains unknown. Thus the extension accommodates the
different information supplied by means and by travel distributions.

\subsection{Perturbing weights}
For each preference centre $w'$, we draw
$w^{(b)}\sim\operatorname{Dirichlet}(\kappa w')$, with
$\kappa\in\{20,100,500\}$ and 10,000 draws per setting. Every draw recomputes
interval TOPSIS and maximum regret without filling missing values. We report
mean decision rank, fractional first-place recommendation frequency and
fractional top-three decision membership; ties share membership.

\input{task4_v154_assets/preferences.tex}



At baseline concentration 100, Oslo has a smaller sample mean decision rank (2.1092)
than Zurich (2.2620), while Zurich has the greater sample recommendation
frequency and is the fixed-weight minimax choice. These summaries describe
different features of the chosen weight distribution. We retain minimum maximum
regret as the predeclared decision rule; weight perturbations report its
sensitivity. A low AHP consistency ratio checks calculation properties,
not representative student opinion.

Numerical precision can be quantified separately from evidence uncertainty.
For $n=10{,}000$ independent, identically distributed draws within each setting,
Hoeffding's inequality and a union bound over the $m=2064$ exported means across
37 settings give a common event of probability at least $0.95$ with error
at most $R\sqrt{\log(2m/0.05)/(2n)}$, where the response range $R$ is 1 for
frequencies and 15 for ranks \cite{hoeffding}.
The resulting half-widths are 2.38 percentage points and 0.357 ranks;
shared draws across outputs are allowed. On this event, subtracting two mean
intervals bounds their difference. Oslo minus Zurich at baseline concentration
100 gives $[-0.867,0.561]$, leaving their expected mean-rank order unresolved
by this guarantee. These bounds concern integration under the declared
simulation laws.

\subsection{Within-city fairness and growth stages}
Citywide facilities per resident measure supply. Student access additionally
depends on fees, opening hours, transport, disability access and location;
identifying its distribution requires matched students and qualifying facilities.

\EquityCase
For districts $d$ with pupil counts $n_d$ and pupil--teacher ratios $r_d$,
the pupil-weighted utility is
$\bar u=\sum_d n_d\,15/(15+r_d)/\sum_dn_d$.
Its difference from pooled utility reflects aggregation; school-location data
establish neither resident travel access nor policy effects.

For Istanbul's retained 2012 high-school cohort aged 14--18, let $L_{0.2}$
be the lowest-20\% weighted mean travel utility, with fractional boundary
inclusion, and $\mu$ the overall mean. Unknown utilities lie in $[0,1]$; optional membership remains uncertain. Observed weight is $W=499950$; including all
optional members gives $N_{\max}=681503$. The sharp joint bound is
$\min(\mu-L_{0.2})=(W/N_{\max})(\mu_{\mathrm{obs}}-L_{0.2,\mathrm{obs}})=0.226686$,
where ``obs'' denotes observed trips.
Including all optional members and setting unknown trips to 45 minutes attains it.
With cohort, weights and missing-data rules fixed, allowing arbitrary utility
changes to at most 5\% or 20\% of observed weighted mass leaves sharp minimum gaps
0.200134 or 0.129091, respectively. This fractional-mass relaxation uses declared
error budgets, not estimated error rates or fractions of students.
The official-portal aggregate projection, rather than original CSV bytes,
supports these historical feasibility results, not current facility-use estimates
\cite{istanbul}.
