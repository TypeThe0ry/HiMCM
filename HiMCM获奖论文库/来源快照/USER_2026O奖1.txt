Location Is Destiny: A Four-Stage MCDM Framework for
Selecting Sustainable Mega-events Host Cities
Large-scale sport mega-events like the Super Bowl present a significant environmental challenge, with
sustainability outcomes critically dependent on the selection of the host city. This paper moves beyond
superficialoperationalfixestoaddressthiscorestrategicissuebydevelopingacomprehensivemathematical
model for selecting the most environmentally sustainable host city. Our approach is centered on a novel
Four-Stage Assessment Framework(Supply, Consumption, Generation, Capacity) that deconstructs an
event’s environmental footprint into a quantifiable, location-dependent process.
To operationalize this framework, we constructed a robustMulti-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM)
model. First, we established a system of twelve secondary indicators, quantifying each using data from
authoritative public sources. We then developed aCombined Weighting Model, integrating the subjective
Analytic Network Process (ANP) with the objective Entropy Weight Method to produce a balanced and
scientifically grounded set of indicator weights. The final sustainability ranking for 22 candidate cities was
then calculated using theTOPSIS method. Our baseline model demonstrated high validity, aligning with
real-world NFL decisions by ranking the San Francisco Bay Area (host of Super Bowl LX) as the second
most sustainable option.
To provide a forward-looking recommendation for the 2029 Super Bowl, we incorporated aTrend Extrap-
olation Correction Model. By projecting policy-driven changes in key indicators like Grid Cleanliness
and Waste Management, we forecasted the 2029 sustainability landscape. The analysis revealed distinct
"archetypes" of sustainability—from the inherently green "All-Rounder" to the policy-driven "Improver."
Afterapplyingareal-world"cooling-offperiod"constraint,ourmodelrecommendsSeattle,WA,asthepre-
mier choice among new potential hosts due to its outstanding all-around performance, and theLos Angeles
Areaas the best option among experienced hosts, driven by its aggressive clean energy policies.
Finally, the model was extended to evaluate multi-venue mega-events like the Olympics, demonstrating its
versatility. Our work provides the NFL with a transparent, data-driven, and strategically insightful tool for
making future host city selections that are genuinely aligned with long-term environmental goals.

1 Introduction and Problem Restatement
1.1 Background
Large-scale sport mega-events like the Olympic Games and the Super Bowl are defining features of our
global culture, generating substantial socio-economic benefits. However, these events also leave a sig-
nificant environmental footprint. Local ecosystems suffer immense pressure from the intensive resource
consumption, large-scale construction, and concentrated global travel required for these events. Although
sustainability has become a key focus for event organizers due to the rapid growth of global environmental
awareness, a considerable gap often persists between sustainability commitments and actual environmental
outcomes, highlighting the need for more scientific and transparent planning tools.
The NFL’s Super Bowl provides a perfect case study for this challenge. As a single-venue event that
movestoanewlocationannually,itallowsustoisolateandstudyacritical,yetoftenunderestimated,variable
in the sustainability equation:the host city. An event’s environmental impact is not a fixed attribute; the
specific context of its location fundamentally shapes it. The carbon intensity of the stadium’s electricity,
the efficiency of public transportation for fans, and the capacity of local water and waste systems are all
determined by the host city’s unique geographical and infrastructural characteristics.
Thisleadstoakeyinsight: achievingtruesustainabilityrequiresashiftinperspective. Insteadofrelying
on temporary fixes, such as adding solar panels to a stadium in a city powered by a coal-dominant grid,
we need a more systematic approach to sustainability. The most impactful decision in making an event
sustainableischoosingahostcitythatalreadypossessesarobustandresilientenvironmentalinfrastructure.
Thechallenge,therefore,isnotjusttomaketheeventlookgreen,buttoselectalocationwheretheeventcan
seamlessly integrate with an already sustainable urban system. This paper aims to develop a mathematical
model that places this environmental logic at the very core of the host city selection process.

4.1 A Four-Stage Framework for Environmental Impact Assessment
To systematically analyze the location-dependent variability of a mega-event’s environmental footprint, we
conceptualize its impact as a linear process that unfolds across four distinct stages:Supply,Consumption,
Generation, andAbsorption. This framework allows us to deconstruct the complex web of interactions
between the event and its host city, moving from the city’s inherent characteristics to the event’s direct
outputs and the environment’s ability to respond. Each stage is defined as follows:
•Stage1: CleanResourceSupply.Thisinitialstageassessesthehostcity’sfoundationalinfrastructure
andresourcebase. Itevaluatestheinherent"greenness"oftheresourcestheeventwilldrawupon,such
as the local electricity grid’s carbon intensity and the efficiency of its public transportation network.
A city with a cleaner supply base provides a more sustainable starting point, regardless of the event’s
specific operations.
•Stage 2: Operational Resource Consumption.This stage quantifies the direct demand the event
places on local resources, a demand that is heavily influenced by the host city’s specific geographical
and climatic context. It focuses on how local conditions, like climate, drive the consumption of

resources such as energy for heating or cooling, and how factors like regional water scarcity amplify
the environmental cost of water usage.
•Stage 3: Event-Generated Load.This stage measures the direct environmental outputs—emissions
andwaste—producedbytheevent’sactivities. Itaccountsfortheprimarypollutionsources,including
themassivecarbonfootprintfromspectatortravelandthesignificantvolumeofsolidwastegenerated
on-site. Thescaleofthisloadisdeterminedbyboththeevent’snatureandthehostcity’scharacteristics.
•Stage 4: Environmental Load-Bearing Capacity.The final stage evaluates the ability of the host
city’s natural and built environment to absorb, mitigate, and recover from the loads generated in the
previousstage. Thisincludestheecosystem’sresilience,measuredbyfactorslikebaselineairquality,
the presence of green spaces, and the region’s long-term vulnerability to environmental shocks like
droughts.

7.3 Strategies for Cities to Keep Long-term High Sustainability Scores
Considering that in real-world circumstances, environmental factors alone cannot determine the sustain-
ability level, because economy is also an important factor. Therefore, the strategies should consider both
factors to work in the real world. Thus we introduce the concept ofcircular economy principles: an
economic model that transforms waste into resources by designing out waste, keeping materials in use, and
regenerating natural systems.
To achieve long-term high sustainability scores, cities can embedcircular economy principlesacross all
four stages of event management. In theSupply Stage, prioritizeregenerative material sourcingand
design events using modular, reusable components. Implementproduct-as-a-service models, which is
leasing audio equipment and LED displays rather than purchasing, ensuring manufacturers maintain and
upgrade the equipment for repeated use. For instance, Super Bowl venues could rent temporary seating
systems that are reinstalled at subsequent events.
DuringConsumptionandGeneration, establishclosed-loopsystemswherewastestreamsbecomeinputs
for other processes. That is, converting food waste from concession stands into compost for urban parks, or
recycling plastic bottles collected during events into construction materials.
In theAbsorption Stage, invest innatural system regenerationthrough urban forestry and green infras-
tructure that enhances ecological carrying capacity. To be specific, planting native tree species around
stadiums to absorb carbon emissions calculated in our model can be helpful.
This systemic approach transforms linear resource flows intoregenerative cycles, ensuring environmen-
tal and economic benefits reinforce each other through continuous material valorization and ecosystem
restoration.
